The rules

One shared tide governs both players. Nobody controls it, it is the only clock in the game, and it runs the economy.

Setup

  • Both players start at 25 life and draw 4 cards.
  • The game opens at low tide.
  • Hand cap 8; drawing past it discards the drawn card.
  • The reef holds 6 cards a side.
  • Player 1 opens without drawing (everyone still starts with the same hand size); player 2's first turn is compensated with +1 energy, to offset the tempo of moving second.

The turn

  • Take your income, then draw one card.
  • Play cards and attack in any order.
  • End your turn. After both players have gone, the round ends.
  • An empty deck means fatigue: 1 damage, then 2, then 3, climbing every draw.

Energy

  • You open on a base capacity of 2, and it rises by 1 per complete tide cycle — not per round — to a ceiling of 10. Capacity is scarce on purpose: the reef is meant to be your engine.
  • The phase pays on top: 0 on a drained flat, +2 on the flood, +1 at high water, 0 on the drain.
  • Unspent energy carries over, up to 3. Bank through a lean phase, dump on the flood.
  • Cards on your board that generate energy add theirs at the start of your turn.
  • Your conservation pile pays +1 every turn per 1 distinct taxon in it — a fish, a coral, a crab and a shark are four; four different fish are one.

Conservation

  • A species that has survived a complete tide cycle on your reef can be released back to the wild.
  • It leaves the board for good into your conservation pile — neither board nor discard, and the one place a card leaves play as an asset.
  • Only 1 goes back per turn, and releasing frees the board slot it held.
  • The pile scores distinct taxa, not names: biodiversity is measured across branches of the tree, so a pile of six reef fish is one branch protected. Protect 3 of the 7 taxa in the set and you win outright.
  • The taxa: Bony fishes, Crustaceans, Molluscs, Cartilaginous fishes, Reptiles, Cnidarians, Echinoderms.

The tide turns

  • It advances once per round, not once per turn, so both players act inside the same phase.
  • Per-turn advancement is configurable, but it locks each player out of two of the four phases.
  • A phase change re-stats every card on the board at once.

Combat

  • A card cannot attack the turn it is played unless it has surge.
  • One attack per card per turn. Cards with 0 attack cannot attack at all.
  • The defender always strikes back, for its full attack plus any spines. Killing something is a trade, which is what stops a wide board clearing everything you play for free.
  • A reef-guard must be dealt with before anything behind it, face included.

Armour

Now that every defender hits back, the armed animals are not the ones that punish an attacker — they are the ones that are hard to hurt. Armour is a printed number that comes off the top of every hit they take: a pufferfish inflated into a ball, an urchin wedged in its socket, an anemone withdrawn.

It applies to attacks and to retaliation alike, so an armoured animal is both awful to attack and awful to be attacked by. Damage never goes below zero: a blocked hit is nothing, not a heal.

Spines

Retaliation is the defender's attack — which is nothing for an urchin, an anemone or a coral head. Universal retaliation quietly made the reef's walls worse rather than better, because attacking them was free again.

Spines is the answer for animals with no attack to answer with. It is dealt back on top of whatever the defender returns by fighting, so the rule is still one rule: a defender returns its attack plus its spines. Printed only on the unarmed — the urchin and the anemone.

Dash

Some species do something the moment they land — a strike, a sweep, a heal, energy, a card. It resolves immediately, before anything else happens, which is what lets a card answer a board rather than merely join one and wait a turn to be killed.

A dash is not a free attack: the card still cannot attack the turn it is played unless it has surge. Not every card has one, by design — a card without a dash is paying for it in stats, in its tide line, or in an aura.

Exposure

  • Some cards are exposed in some phases — stranded, bleaching, out of their element.
  • An exposed card takes +1 damage from every attack against it, and from retaliation.
  • Kill a toxic animal in combat, from either side of the bite, and whatever landed the blow dies too. Wounding one costs nothing extra, and a predator printed toxin-immune kills it and survives.
  • So attacking with a stranded card into anything that can answer is punished twice.
  • Exposure is a window, not a state: it opens and closes with the tide.

Damage and death

Tide and symbiosis effects are continuous — a card's stats are whatever the current phase and its current neighbours say they are. Damage, by contrast, is marked permanently on the card.

So a card damaged at high tide can drown when the tide falls and lowers its ceiling below the damage already on it. No one attacked it; the water simply left. Killing a partner does the same thing, which is why deaths cascade.

Winning

  • Reduce your opponent to 0 life.
  • If both players hit zero at once, the reef takes them both — a draw.

Symbiosis

Cards carry biological traits, and some carry auras that grant stats to friendly cards with a given trait. Mutualism is not a special case: it is simply both partners carrying an aura pointed at the other. Auras never apply to the card itself, and never reach across to the opponent.

A printed armour value comes off the top of every hit they take, from attacks and from retaliation alike. All three are also toxic: kill one and whatever landed the blow dies of the toxin, unless it is one of the few predators printed as toxin-immune.

Structures — the reef itself

They do not attack. They hold ground, pay energy on the flood and at high water, shelter the fish that live in them, and bake when the tide goes out — every one of them is exposed at low tide. Several also build into each other: a frame-builder aura reads any other frame-builder on the board, so a wall of them is worth more than the sum of its cards — the closest thing in the set to a reef being a structure rather than a pile of cards.
